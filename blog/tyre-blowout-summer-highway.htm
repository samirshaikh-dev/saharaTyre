Tyre Blowout on Highway: The Real Cause Is Under-Inflation, Not Heat

By Sahara Tyre Technical Team • Updated October 2026 • 6 min read

Almost every driver who has a tyre blowout in an Indian summer has the same explanation ready, and it is the wrong one. They will say the heat got too much for the tyre. If that were true, every properly inflated tyre on the road would fail every April. It does not. What actually happens is a combination of an under-inflated starting pressure, sustained high-speed flexing, and internal damage that was already there.

boltDirect Answer

Under-inflation is the number one cause of summer blowouts. A tyre at 6 PSI below specification flexes excessively on every rotation at highway speed, which generates heat faster than the rubber can dissipate it. Internal temperatures pass 100 degrees Celsius, the rubber compound starts to break down, and in a tyre that already had cord damage from a pothole, the sidewall finally fails catastrophically. Heat alone will not destroy a correctly inflated tyre. The single most effective prevention is a cold pressure check every month, set to the door-jamb placard value, never the number moulded into the sidewall.

The 3 PSI Myth That Causes Summer Blowouts

Drivers in Vapi and throughout Gujarat routinely release air from hot tyres in summer because they believe the extra pressure will make the tyre pop. This is the exact opposite of safe practice. A tyre rolling at 100 km/h flexes continuously, and that flexing heats the internal air, raising pressure by 4 to 6 PSI above the cold reading on its own. That rise is normal and expected. If you bleed air to compensate, you end up at the roadside with a tyre genuinely below specification, and you have converted a safe tyre into a dangerous one. The rule is absolute: pressure is only ever set while the tyre is cold, meaning the vehicle has been parked at least three hours or driven under two kilometres at low speed.

The Real Failure Mechanism, Step by Step

  1. A tyre starts under-inflated. Small leaks, natural permeation through ageing butyl, and a missed pressure check are the usual sources. Below specification the sidewall and shoulder deflect further than designed on every rotation.
  2. Repeated flexing generates shear heat. The excessive sidewall deflection creates frictional heat inside the casing that grows the faster you drive.
  3. Internal temperature passes the safe threshold. Rubber compounds begin to stiffen, then break down. Internal casing temperatures can exceed 100 degrees Celsius in this state.
  4. Existing damage becomes critical. If the tyre already carries cord damage from a pothole pinch or was run flat, the weakened section can no longer hold. Belt edge separation follows.
  5. The tyre fails suddenly. The rupture is usually a sidewall or shoulder tear, not a tread blowout, and it happens at highway speed with no warning shot.

Why Nitrogen Helps More Than Most People Expect

Filling with dry nitrogen rather than ambient compressed air slows pressure loss substantially, because nitrogen molecules are around four times larger than oxygen and diffuse through the butyl inner liner three to four times more slowly. That matters directly for blowout prevention, because a stable pressure means a stable flexing profile. Dry nitrogen also contains no water vapour, which removes the biggest single contributor to heat-driven pressure spikes on long highway runs, and it eliminates internal rim corrosion. For commercial operators running NH 48 at sustained 90 to 100 km/h in April, this is one of the cheapest risk reductions available. At Sahara Tyre every tyre we fit leaves with an initial nitrogen fill and a green valve cap so you can identify it later.

Where the Sidewall Max Pressure Marking Misleads You

Every tyre sidewall is stamped with a MAX PRESSURE figure, frequently around 350 kPa or 51 PSI. That number is the maximum cold inflation limit the tyre is certified for under load, not a target pressure. Your vehicle manufacturer set a specific operating pressure on the door-jamb placard, often noticeably lower, because that value is a compromise between tyre life, ride comfort, fuel economy, braking and handling. Using the max figure is one of the most common causes of centre-rib wear, harsh ride and puncture risk from crown cuts. Always inflate to the placard, and treat the sidewall maximum as a ceiling you should never approach.

Exact Steps If a Tyre Bursts on NH 48

  1. Do not slam the brakes. Sudden braking transfers all weight forward and can spin the vehicle. Grip the wheel firmly with both hands at the nine and three o'clock positions.
  2. Ease off the accelerator progressively. Let engine braking slow the car below roughly 40 km/h. The failed tyre drags slightly but it is manageable.
  3. Keep the vehicle tracking straight. Do not make sharp steering inputs.
  4. Move onto the left shoulder or service road. Signal early, then apply the handbrake once fully stopped with wheels turned away from traffic.
  5. Switch on hazard warning flashers. Place the reflective triangle at least 50 metres behind the vehicle facing oncoming traffic.
  6. Move all passengers behind the crash barrier. Exit from the side away from traffic.

Prevention Is a 15-Minute Monthly Job

A cold pressure check with a quality gauge, tread depth measurement on all four tyres, and a walk around the car looking at sidewalls takes fifteen minutes and prevents most of this. Do it in the morning before driving. Add a tyre rotation every 8,000 to 10,000 km, because uneven wear concentrates heat in one tyre, and a worn tyre runs hotter than a full-depth one at identical pressure. If you drive NH 48 regularly, a pre-summer inspection in March or April that switches the tyres to nitrogen is well worth it. Frequently Asked Questions

Why do my tyres blow out more in summer? expand_more

Because most tyres that fail in summer were already under-inflated. Under-inflation causes excessive sidewall flexing at highway speed, which generates heat faster than the casing can dissipate it. Sustained high-speed driving adds a normal 4 to 6 PSI heat rise, so a tyre that started 6 PSI low ends up running very soft and very hot. Heat alone does not destroy a correctly inflated tyre.

Should I release air from hot tyres in summer? expand_more

Absolutely not. This is the most damaging habit in Indian summer tyre maintenance. Heat-generated pressure rise of 4 to 6 PSI is normal. Bleeding air leaves the tyre genuinely under-inflated once it cools, which increases heat, flexing and blowout risk. Always set pressure on a cold tyre, parked at least three hours.

What pressure should I use, the door jamb or the sidewall marking? expand_more

Use the door-jamb placard value. The sidewall maximum, often around 51 PSI, is a certified cold ceiling rather than a target. Over-inflating to the sidewall maximum causes centre-rib wear, a harsh ride, reduced braking contact and a much higher risk of crown cuts and punctures.

Does nitrogen prevent blowouts? expand_more

It reduces the risk meaningfully but does not eliminate it. Nitrogen diffuses far more slowly than compressed air, so pressure stays stable, and dry nitrogen contains no water vapour, which removes the largest contributor to heat-driven pressure spikes. A correctly inflated tyre in nitrogen still flexes far less than an under-inflated one, so the pressure check still matters more than the gas.

What should I do if a tyre bursts at 100 km/h? expand_more

Do not slam the brakes. Hold the wheel firmly with both hands, ease off the accelerator progressively to let engine braking slow the car below 40 km/h, keep it tracking straight, then move onto the left shoulder. Apply the handbrake, switch on hazard flashers, place a reflective triangle at least 50 metres behind the vehicle, move passengers behind the barrier and call roadside assistance.

Can a heat-damaged tyre be repaired? expand_more

It depends entirely on whether internal rubber has separated from the belt, which cannot be seen from outside. If internal casing temperatures exceeded around 100 degrees Celsius, the compound has degraded permanently and the tyre must be replaced. We will not patch a heat-damaged tyre because we cannot honestly certify it. Bring it in and we will show you the inner liner condition.
