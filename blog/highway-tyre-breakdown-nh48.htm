Tyre Breakdown on NH 48: What to Do in the First 60 Seconds

By Sahara Tyre Roadside Assistance Team • Updated October 2026 • 7 min read

NH 48 through Vapi carries over 50,000 vehicles a day, freight and passenger both, at 100 km/h. A tyre failure there is survivable, but only if the first sixty seconds are handled well. Almost all serious injuries on this highway come from secondary collisions and from drivers who stopped in a live lane, not from the puncture itself.

What Causes a Blowout on the NH 48 Corridor

Knowing the cause helps you judge the risk before you move. A blowout means the casing has failed catastrophically, usually from under-inflation combined with heat, or from an existing internal injury from an earlier pothole impact that finally gave way. If you felt the car wobbling or pulling in the minutes before it went, suspect internal damage and do not remount the old tyre, whatever the tread looks like. A slow puncture is more forgiving. If you felt a sharp thump and then progressive pressure loss, you likely still have some control and may be able to limp a short distance to a safe spot at low speed. Either way, get off the carriageway first.

The highway itself is not kind to tyres. Expansion joints, debris from heavy vehicles, and general road surface wear are constant. The 2 to 3 PSI steady loss people complain about after fitting is often not a puncture at all but the natural permeation of air through the inner liner. It is a reason to check pressure monthly, and it is a reason never to keep driving a low tyre down a highway.

The 7-Step Response, in Order

  1. Hold the wheel firmly. Both hands at nine and three o'clock. Small corrections only. Do not fight a sudden pull with a large steering input.
  2. Ease off the accelerator progressively. Do not brake hard. Engine braking will slow you down smoothly without locking wheels. Aim to get below 40 km/h before applying the brakes.
  3. Keep tracking straight. Do not slam on the brakes. Sudden braking transfers weight forward, which can spin the vehicle or cause a rollover on a loaded truck.
  4. Signal and move to the shoulder. Use your indicator to signal left, then guide the vehicle fully onto the left paved shoulder or adjacent service road. Never stop in a live lane.
  5. Switch on hazard flashers. This tells following traffic that you are stationary and slow, regardless of your indicator.
  6. Place the reflective triangle 50 metres behind the vehicle, facing oncoming traffic. This gives the vehicles behind you, which are travelling faster than you expect, time to react.
  7. Get passengers out and behind the barrier. Exit from the side away from traffic, the left on an Indian highway. Have everyone stand behind the crash barrier if there is one. Do not stand between the vehicle and the traffic lane.

What Goes in the Trunk, Every Time

A short roadside survival kit costs very little and decides how bad a breakdown is. Carry a calibrated tyre pressure gauge, either analog or digital. Carry a hydraulic bottle jack or scissors jack rated above your vehicle curb weight. Carry a telescopic lug wrench with the correct socket size, 17 mm, 19 mm or 21 mm depending on your vehicle. Carry a reflective safety vest so you are visible at night. Carry a portable 12V cigarette-lighter socket air inflator. Carry a heavy pair of gloves and a waterproof LED torch. Trucks and Loaded Vehicles on NH 48

A loaded commercial vehicle has different priorities in a blowout, because of inertia. Do not brake hard, as the load will shift and can jackknife the trailer. Keep it in a straight line, apply the engine retarder or progressive engine braking, and get as far off the running lane as possible, even onto the shoulder or a lay-by. Once stopped, warn traffic and set up your triangle well back. Because commercial tyres run at 100 to 130 PSI, never attempt to inflate or refit one yourself, a rim failure at those pressures is genuinely dangerous. Call for professional help.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do first if my tyre bursts on NH 48? expand_more

Hold the wheel firmly with both hands, ease off the accelerator progressively to let engine braking slow you below 40 km/h, keep tracking straight, then guide the vehicle onto the left shoulder. Do not slam the brakes, because sudden braking transfers weight forward and can spin the vehicle. Signal left early, then apply the handbrake once fully stopped.

How far behind should I place the warning triangle? expand_more

At least 50 metres behind the vehicle, positioned to face oncoming traffic. This gives faster-moving following vehicles time to react, which matters more than it sounds when you have just been forced off a lane at speed.

Can I drive a truck on a blown tyre to the nearest workshop? expand_more

Only if the tyre is retaining pressure and you can keep below 40 km/h with full control. If the tyre is fully deflated, the rim will be riding on the road surface, which destroys the bead and rim and can cause a rim failure. At commercial pressures, never attempt to remount a wheel yourself. Call for professional roadside assistance instead.

What causes most blowouts on Indian highways? expand_more

Under-inflation is the leading cause. A tyre 6 PSI below specification flexes excessively on every rotation, generating heat until internal temperatures exceed 100 degrees Celsius, at which point an already damaged casing fails catastrophically. A pothole pinch from a previous impact that caused internal cord damage is usually the underlying pre-existing fault that makes a correctly inflated tyre fail.

Should I remount the old tyre after a highway blowout? expand_more

No. A tyre that has run flat, or that has overheated to the point of failing, has internal damage that cannot be seen from outside. Remounting it risks another failure, possibly on the next highway trip. We will inspect it and show you the inner liner condition, and advise replacement.
