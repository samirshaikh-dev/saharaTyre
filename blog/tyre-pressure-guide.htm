Tyre Pressure Guide: The Correct PSI for Your Car and How to Set It

By Sahara Tyre Technical Team • Updated October 2026 • 7 min read

Tyre pressure is the cheapest performance setting on your vehicle and the most neglected one. A pressure out by 6 PSI costs you measurable fuel economy, measurably shorter tyre life, and measurably longer braking distances. Yet most drivers never know their correct number, because they assume the value moulded into the sidewall is the one to use. It is not.

boltDirect Answer

Always set pressure from the placard on the driver door frame, the fuel filler flap, or the owner manual, and never from the sidewall maximum. The sidewall figure, typically around 350 kPa or 51 PSI, is a certified cold ceiling, not a target. Most Indian hatchback placards read 30 to 33 PSI front and rear, compact SUVs 32 to 33, and heavy commercial tubeless radials 115 to 125. Pressure is only ever set cold: park for three hours or drive under two kilometres, because highway flexing adds 4 to 6 PSI of normal heat-driven rise.

Where to Find Your Correct PSI: 3 Places, in Order of Priority

  1. The driver door frame placard. This is the manufacturer figure for your exact model and variant, and it is always the correct answer. It is usually a small sticker on the door jamb, visible when the door is open.
  2. The fuel filler flap. Many manufacturers repeat the placard here. It is convenient but occasionally out of date on older cars, so cross-check against the door jamb.
  3. The owner manual. The authoritative fallback, and the place to find the split front and rear figures plus the full-load and stepney values.

The sidewall is not on this list. The number moulded into your tyre, marked MAX PRESSURE or MAX LOAD, is the highest cold pressure the tyre is certified to withstand at maximum rated load. It is a ceiling, not a recommendation. Inflate to it and you will wear out the centre rib, ride harshly, lose braking contact patch, and greatly increase your risk of a crown cut. Many drivers assume the higher number is the safer choice. It is the less safe one.

Cold Pressure: What It Means and Why the Definition Matters

Cold means the vehicle has been parked for at least three hours, or driven under two kilometres at low speed. Driving heats the air inside the tyre, and a tyre rolling at 100 km/h flexes continuously, generating friction heat that raises pressure by 4 to 6 PSI on its own. That rise is normal and expected. If you measure a hot tyre at 36 PSI, it may be perfectly correct at 32 PSI cold. This is exactly why the habit of bleeding air from hot tyres in summer is so damaging: it leaves you genuinely under-inflated once the tyre cools. Check first thing in the morning, before driving, on a level surface.

How to Check Pressure Correctly in 6 Steps

  1. Park on level ground, switch off the engine, and wait at least three hours. Do not check after a drive.
  2. Use a good quality gauge. A pencil-style gauge reads far less accurately than a digital or oil-damped dial gauge, and an under-reading gauge means you under-inflate.
  3. Remove the valve cap. If the gauge shows more than 3 PSI above the placard, bleed air down to the target rather than equalising to the highest reading.
  4. Check all four tyres plus the stepney. Owners routinely check three and forget the fourth, which is how one wheel ends up running 8 PSI low.
  5. Check pressure on all four together. Equal pressure is as important as correct pressure.
  6. Check the spare monthly if it is a full-size wheel, and keep it 3 to 5 PSI above operating pressure to offset storage loss. If it is a temporary space-saver, check it every few months and confirm the repair kit and compressor are present.

What Under-Inflation Does, and What Over-Inflation Does

Running Low

  • arrow_forwardCentre tread collapses inward, shifting the contact patch onto both shoulders, which wear out 30 to 40 percent faster.
  • arrow_forwardRolling resistance rises 10 to 15 percent, cutting fuel economy by roughly 5 to 8 percent.
  • arrow_forwardSidewall flexing generates internal heat above 100 degrees Celsius, the primary cause of summer blowouts.
  • arrow_forwardThe rim edge can pinch the sidewall against a pothole, creating an impact bulge.
  • arrow_forwardHydroplaning onset speed drops by 15 to 20 km/h because the deformed tread cannot evacuate water.

Running High

  • arrow_forwardThe crown bows outward and carries a disproportionate share of vehicle weight, so the centre rib wears out first.
  • arrow_forwardContact patch shrinks, lengthening braking distances on wet and dry tarmac alike.
  • arrow_forwardRide harshness increases and steering response becomes sharper, which reduces grip on wet surfaces.
  • arrow_forwardThe crown sits further from the rim, so it deflects more over sharp edges and cuts are far more likely.
  • arrow_forwardBead and rim stress rises, which is how over-inflation contributes to bead failures.

If your tread shows fast wear in the centre, check for over-inflation. If both shoulders are worn flat and the centre still has depth, check for under-inflation. That pattern check is more reliable than most drivers expect, and it costs nothing.

Nitrogen vs Compressed Air, and the TPMS Question

Nitrogen molecules are roughly four times larger than oxygen and diffuse through a butyl liner three to four times more slowly, so pressure holds far longer. Dry nitrogen also contains no water vapour, which removes the largest single cause of heat-driven pressure spikes on long highway runs, and it eliminates internal rim corrosion. Topping up a nitrogen-filled tyre with ordinary air in an emergency is completely safe, since ambient air is already 78 percent nitrogen; you simply lose the purity benefits until the next purge. Most drivers need a top-up every two to three months, not a full purge, unless air was mixed in.

If your vehicle has TPMS, do not ignore the dashboard warning. A pressure 20 percent below specification triggers it well before you would notice a handling change. Note that TPMS typically reports the tyre pressure and compares it to a fixed threshold rather than the placard value, so it is a warning system, not a substitute for a gauge.

Frequently Asked Questions

What PSI should my car tyres be? expand_more

Use the placard on the driver door frame, the fuel filler flap, or the owner manual for your exact model. Most Indian hatchbacks run 30 to 33 PSI, sedans 32 to 33, compact SUVs 32 to 33, and heavy commercial tubeless radials 115 to 125. Never use the MAX PRESSURE figure moulded on the sidewall, which is a certified ceiling and not a target.

What is cold tyre pressure? expand_more

Pressure measured when the vehicle has been parked at least three hours or driven under two kilometres at low speed. Highway flexing heats the air and raises pressure by 4 to 6 PSI, which is normal. Measure in the morning before driving so the reading reflects the true cold pressure.

How often should I check tyre pressure? expand_more

Monthly at minimum, and before every long highway trip. Pressures lose roughly 1 PSI per month naturally, faster in hot climates. Also check all four together plus the spare, since owners routinely check three wheels and miss a fourth running 8 PSI low. If your vehicle has TPMS, treat it as a warning system rather than a replacement for a gauge.

Should I increase tyre pressure in the rain? expand_more

Do not decrease it. Many drivers lower pressure in the monsoon on the belief a softer tyre grips better. The opposite is true: an under-inflated tyre flexes inward, narrows the contact patch, and traps water, lowering aquaplaning onset speed by 15 to 20 km/h. Set cold pressure at the placard value, or about 2 PSI above it during monsoon months.

What happens if I drive with over-inflated tyres? expand_more

The crown bows outward and carries a disproportionate share of weight, so the centre rib wears out first. Contact patch shrinks, lengthening braking distances. Ride harshness increases, the tyre is more exposed to sharp objects and cuts, and bead and rim stress rises. Over-inflation is not the safe choice many drivers assume it is.

Is it safe to mix normal air with nitrogen in my tyres? expand_more

Yes, entirely. Compressed ambient air is already about 78 percent nitrogen, so topping up a nitrogen-filled tyre with regular air causes no chemical harm. You only lose the benefits of higher purity, mainly slower pressure loss and no moisture. Top up whenever needed and schedule a full purge and refill at your next annual service.
