Truck Tyre Maintenance for Fleets: Dual Matching, Torque and Cost Control

By Sahara Tyre Fleet Services Team • Updated October 2026 • 8 min read

A commercial fleet does not fail because of one bad tyre. It fails because of a systematic gap: one tyre in a dual pair is slightly smaller than its partner, so it runs hotter; a wheel nut loses tension after 200 km; a casing gets run down to the steel belts before anyone schedules it for retreading. Each of those is cheap to prevent and expensive to ignore. This is the maintenance discipline that keeps transport fleets in Vapi GIDC running without roadside downtime.

Five practices define professional fleet tyre management. Dual tyres must be matched within 6 mm in diameter and 19 mm in circumference, with the larger tyre on the outside because road camber makes the inner tyre carry more load. Wheel nuts must be torqued in a crisscross sequence to 550 to 650 Nm with a calibrated wrench and re-checked after the first 100 km. Any inflation above 100 PSI must happen inside a certified safety cage. Casings must be pulled for retreading at 2.5 to 3.0 mm remaining tread, before the steel breaker belt is destroyed. And a daily cold pressure audit on every vehicle leaving the yard. Miss these and cost per kilometre rises faster than fuel prices do.

Rule 1: Dual-Tyre Height Matching Within 6 mm

On a tandem dual axle, the two tyres must be matched closely in size. The maximum permitted difference is 6 mm in diameter, about a quarter inch, which corresponds to 19 mm in circumference, about three quarters of an inch. Why so tight? If one tyre is meaningfully larger, it carries over 70 percent of the axle payload. That larger tyre runs hotter, its shoulders wear unevenly, its casing fatigues early, and braking distance on that corner becomes unpredictable. The smaller tyre carries too little load and scuffs its shoulders against the road instead of rolling.

When a slight difference exists, the slightly larger tyre goes on the outside position. This surprises people, but it follows from road camber: arterial roads are built with a lateral slope for drainage, so the inner tyre in a dual assembly carries slightly more weight. Putting the larger tyre inside would overload the side already carrying more. Never mate a bias-ply tyre with a radial on the same axle, and never pair a brand-new tyre with a deeply worn one in the same dual assembly.

Rule 2: Crisscross Lug Torque to 550-650 Nm, Then Re-check

Truck and trailer wheel nuts must always be tightened in a crisscross star sequence, not around the circle in order. Sequential tightening leaves one nut carrying the full load first, which distorts the hub face and causes brake drum and disc warping. A crisscross pattern distributes clamp force evenly across the hub face, which is what prevents warped discs and uneven braking.

The torque specification for heavy commercial M22 studs is 550 to 650 Nm, roughly 405 to 480 foot-pounds, verified with a calibrated click-type torque wrench. An uncalibrated pneumatic impact gun is not acceptable evidence that a nut was torqued correctly. Then re-torque the wheels after the vehicle completes its first 100 km of loaded travel. Wheel faces settle under load, and that settling causes real torque relaxation, which is why so many wheel-related failures trace back to a nut that was never re-checked.

Rule 3: Safety-Cage Inflation Above 100 PSI

Commercial tyres operate at 100 to 130 PSI depending on construction. At those pressures, a failed tyre is genuinely dangerous to the person inflating it. All commercial inflation must take place inside a certified steel safety cage, using clip-on air chucks with a minimum three-metre inline hose and a pressure regulator, with the technician standing outside the potential trajectory zone of the wheel. This is not optional procedure, and it is the first thing we check when assessing another workshop's commercial bay.

Torque specifications by fastener: M20 studs on heavy commercial trucks take 450 to 500 Nm, M22 ISO studs common on modern multi-axle units take 550 to 650 Nm, and M18 studs on medium commercial LCVs take 300 to 350 Nm. Set valve stems 180 degrees opposite each other on dual wheels so fleet pressure gauges and nitrogen chucks are reachable without dismounting the outer wheel.

Rule 4: Pull Casings at 2.5-3.0 mm, Not at the Legal Limit

This is the single most financially expensive mistake in fleet tyre management. Running a commercial casing down to the 1.6 mm legal limit, or even to 2 mm, destroys the casing. The steel breaker belt becomes exposed, the casing takes an impact from every stone in the road, and the structural integrity that made retreading possible is gone. A casing that could have been retreaded once becomes scrap.

Pull the casing at 2.5 to 3.0 mm remaining tread. Premium all-steel radials from MRF, Apollo and Bridgestone can typically be retreaded two to three times if they have not been run flat, suffered bead damage, or experienced belt separation, extending total casing life to 250,000 to 300,000 kilometres. Cold precure retreading consumes roughly 70 percent less crude oil, 65 percent less energy and 30 percent less rubber than manufacturing a new tyre, which is why an operator who retreads responsibly is not economising on safety, they are using the casing value that was paid for.

Rule 5: A Daily Cold Pressure Audit Before the Vehicle Leaves

A pre-trip pressure audit with an oil-damped dial gauge on every steer, drive and trailer tyre is the cheapest control in the entire programme. Running 10 PSI low increases fuel consumption by about 2.5 percent and accelerates shoulder wear by around 15 percent. The audit takes four minutes per vehicle and it catches slow leaks, damaged valves and heat-related pressure loss before they become roadside failures on NH 48.

Three companion checks complete the daily routine. Inspect the void between dual rear tyres for lodged stones, brick fragments and scrap iron, which cause friction blowouts. Confirm every tyre has a proper high-pressure valve cap, since the valve core is a secondary seal and the cap is the primary defence against road grit and centrifugal air loss. And walk the tread with a gloved hand to catch early feathering or cupping while it is still correctable.

The critical qualifier is quality: that saving assumes a sound, undamaged casing that was inspected properly. We inspect every incoming casing for bead damage, belt separation, run-flat evidence and internal injury before any buffing happens, and we reject anything that does not pass. A retread is only as strong as the casing under it.

Axle Placement Rules for Retreads

Retreads belong on drive axles, tandem rear axles, lift axles and trailer axles. They are restricted from the steering axles of passenger buses and high-speed highway trucks, which must always run brand-new tyres, because emergency directional control at expressway speed depends on the steer axle having the full margin of a new casing. The same logic applies to cars, where we advise brand-new or five-point tested used original radials rather than remoulds.
